Intellectual Property & AI

IP Rights: Why They Matter to Creative Professionals and What Impact Might AI Have?

My thoughts on protecting creative work, understanding copyright and navigating the impact of generative AI on game development.

Introduction

As someone studying Game Design and Development, I have always found the creative side of making games particularly interesting. Whether it involves developing gameplay mechanics, designing characters, writing stories or programming systems, creating something from an original idea can be extremely rewarding.

However, one thing I think is sometimes overlooked is the importance of protecting that work. If I spent months developing a game, I certainly wouldn't appreciate someone copying my assets, taking credit for my work or attempting to profit from something I had created without permission.

This is where intellectual property rights become important.

With the increasing popularity of generative artificial intelligence, I believe the subject has become even more complicated. AI tools can now generate images, code, music and written content in a matter of seconds, raising questions about who owns the results and whether the original creators whose work helped train these systems have been treated fairly.

Personally, I have mixed feelings about generative AI. I can see how it could become a useful tool for developers, particularly when brainstorming ideas or solving technical problems. However, I am also concerned about the potential impact on artists, copyright ownership and the value of human creativity.

So, why do intellectual property rights matter within game development, and could generative AI change how creative work is owned and protected?

What Are Intellectual Property Rights?

Intellectual property, commonly referred to as IP, describes creations of the mind, including artistic works, inventions, designs, software and branding.

According to the UK Intellectual Property Office, intellectual property rights help creators protect their work and control how it is used by other people.

There are several different forms of protection, including copyright, trademarks, patents and design rights.

Copyright protects original creative expressions such as artwork, music, written content and software. Trademarks can protect distinctive branding, including company names and logos. Patents protect qualifying inventions, while design rights can protect aspects of a product's appearance.

I think understanding these differences is particularly important in game development because a single game can contain several forms of intellectual property.

For example, a game's source code, character artwork, soundtrack, title and branding may all involve different legal rights.

As a developer, I believe understanding which protections apply to my work would be useful if I eventually decided to release games commercially or establish an independent studio.

Why Copyright Matters in Game Development

Of the different intellectual property rights, I consider copyright particularly relevant to game development.

Games combine numerous creative elements, including programming, visual artwork, sound effects, music and storytelling.

The UK government's copyright guidance explains that qualifying original works receive copyright protection automatically, without requiring a formal registration process.

I think this is reassuring for smaller developers because it means they do not necessarily need to spend money registering every piece of original artwork or code they create.

However, copyright does not protect every aspect of a game.

One distinction I find particularly important is the difference between an idea and its expression.

For example, I could develop a platformer where the player jumps between platforms, collects objects and avoids enemies. Other developers are generally free to create games using similar broad ideas and mechanics.

However, directly copying another developer's original artwork, music or source code could infringe copyright.

The Intellectual Property Office's guidance on IP basics explains that copyright protects the expression of an idea rather than the underlying idea itself.

Personally, I think this distinction is necessary. If developers could claim exclusive ownership of broad gameplay concepts, creating new games would become extremely difficult.

At the same time, I believe developers deserve protection against people who deliberately copy their original work.

Protecting My Own Projects

When considering intellectual property, I think it is useful to reflect on the games I have created throughout my studies.

For example, Galactic Escape is a mobile endless runner developed using Unity, where players control a spaceship and avoid incoming asteroids.

Although the idea of avoiding obstacles is not unique, the specific combination of original code, artwork and other creative elements could involve copyright-protected material.

Similarly, projects such as Undead Survival and Galaxy Defense contain programming and visual elements that contribute to their individual identities.

I believe it is important to understand which assets I have created myself and which may have been obtained from external sources.

For example, if I downloaded a sound effect or texture from an online asset library, I would need to check the licensing conditions before including it in a published game.

Some assets are available for commercial use, while others require attribution or have restrictions on redistribution.

I also think keeping records of asset sources and licences is good practice.

If I eventually decided to release a game commercially, I would want to be confident that I had permission to use everything included in the finished product.

For collaborative projects, I would also want to establish who owns the resulting work and whether individual contributors have agreed to its publication.

After all, simply contributing to a university project does not automatically mean I can claim ownership of everything created by the entire team.

Intellectual Property Disputes Within the Games Industry

One of the reasons I believe developers should understand intellectual property is that disputes can have serious consequences, even for established companies.

An interesting example involves Nintendo, The Pokémon Company and Pocketpair, the developer of Palworld.

Nintendo and The Pokémon Company filed a patent infringement lawsuit against Pocketpair, alleging that Palworld infringed several patents.

According to Pocketpair's official statement about the lawsuit, the claims concerned three Japanese patents, with the plaintiffs seeking an injunction and financial compensation.

I find this dispute particularly interesting because it demonstrates the difference between copyright and patents.

Although much of the public discussion surrounding Palworld has focused on comparisons with Pokémon, the reported lawsuit concerns patents rather than simply allegations that the games look similar.

I think this is an important distinction because intellectual property disputes are not always as straightforward as they initially appear.

Personally, I understand why companies want to protect technologies and intellectual property they have invested significant resources into developing.

However, I also think overly restrictive protections could create difficulties for smaller developers who want to experiment with familiar gameplay concepts.

I would not want independent developers to feel that creating a game inspired by an existing genre automatically exposes them to legal action.

At the same time, I don't believe developers should be allowed to deliberately copy another company's protected work without consequences.

For me, the challenge is finding a reasonable balance between protecting genuine innovation and allowing creative competition.

Can Intellectual Property Rights Restrict Creativity?

Although I support intellectual property protection, I also think it is worth considering whether these rights can sometimes discourage creativity.

Many games build upon mechanics and ideas introduced by earlier titles.

For example, platformers, role-playing games and first-person shooters all contain conventions that have developed through decades of experimentation.

If every individual gameplay concept could be exclusively controlled by one developer, I think the industry would struggle to evolve.

However, without intellectual property protection, developers might have fewer incentives to invest time and resources into producing original work.

A small independent studio could spend years developing a game, only for another company to reproduce its original assets and profit from them.

I believe that would be extremely unfair.

In my opinion, intellectual property rights should protect meaningful creative contributions without preventing developers from learning from existing games and exploring similar genres.

I think inspiration is an important part of creativity, but there is a difference between being inspired by something and directly copying it.

The Growing Influence of Generative AI

Generative artificial intelligence introduces another layer of complexity to this discussion.

AI systems can generate images, text, code, music and other content based on user instructions.

From a game development perspective, I can understand why these tools are becoming attractive.

For example, an independent developer might use AI to brainstorm gameplay ideas, explore possible storylines or receive suggestions when debugging code.

Personally, I think these applications could be genuinely useful.

As someone who has worked with Unity, GameMaker and different programming languages, I know that development often involves researching unfamiliar problems and experimenting with possible solutions.

I can see AI becoming another tool that helps developers work through these challenges.

However, I believe there is an important difference between using AI to support creativity and allowing it to replace the creative process entirely.

If I asked an AI system to explain why a programming function was failing, I would still need to understand the suggested solution and verify that it worked correctly.

Similarly, if AI helped me brainstorm a game concept, I would still want to make the important design decisions myself.

I don't think generating an entire game through automated tools would provide the same learning experience as understanding how its systems are created.

AI-Generated Artwork: Who Benefits and Who Loses?

One of my biggest concerns about generative AI is its impact on artists.

Game development relies heavily on visual creativity, from character design and environmental artwork to animation and user interfaces.

AI image-generation tools can produce illustrations extremely quickly, which could make them attractive to studios attempting to reduce development costs.

For smaller independent developers with limited budgets, I can understand the appeal.

However, I worry that relying too heavily on AI-generated artwork could reduce opportunities for professional artists.

I also think there are ethical questions surrounding the material used to train these systems.

If an artist's work is used to develop an AI model without their permission, should they have a say in how that material is used?

Should they receive compensation if a commercial product benefits from their creative work?

Personally, I believe creators should have meaningful control over the use of their work.

The UK government's report on copyright and artificial intelligence examines different approaches to AI training, including licensing requirements, exceptions for data mining and measures intended to improve transparency.

The report also acknowledges concerns raised by creative professionals about how their work may be used.

I think these concerns are reasonable, particularly when artists may be competing commercially with systems trained on large collections of existing creative material.

However, I don't believe every use of generative AI is automatically unethical.

For me, the important questions are whether the training material was obtained lawfully, whether creators' rights were respected and how the resulting content is being used.

Who Owns AI-Generated Content?

Another issue I find interesting is determining who actually owns work produced using generative AI.

If someone enters a short prompt and an AI system generates an image, can that person claim to be its creator?

What happens if the generated image resembles an existing copyrighted character?

These questions are particularly important for game developers who might want to use AI-generated assets in commercial projects.

The United States Copyright Office's report on AI-generated works explains that copyright protection in the United States depends on sufficient human authorship.

Its analysis distinguishes between content generated entirely by AI and work where a person has made meaningful creative contributions.

I think this is a sensible distinction, although I recognise that copyright laws differ between countries.

In the UK, the legal position also involves specific provisions concerning computer-generated works, making the situation more complicated than simply saying all AI-generated content is protected or unprotected.

Personally, I would be cautious about relying on AI-generated assets without first understanding the relevant licensing conditions and legal implications.

If I were developing a commercial game, I would want clear documentation showing where the assets came from and what rights I had to use them.

I believe uncertainty over ownership could become a serious problem if a game became commercially successful.

My Personal Opinion on AI in Game Development

Overall, I would describe my opinion on generative AI as cautiously optimistic.

I believe AI has the potential to become a valuable development tool, particularly for brainstorming, technical assistance and repetitive tasks.

For example, I could see myself using AI to help understand programming errors, compare different implementation approaches or generate ideas that I could then develop independently.

However, I would not want to become entirely dependent on it.

One of the reasons I enjoy game development is the satisfaction of solving problems and creating something through my own efforts.

If AI completed every difficult task for me, I think I would lose opportunities to develop my abilities.

I am also concerned about how AI could affect employment within the creative industries.

While automation might make certain production processes more efficient, I believe studios should consider the impact on the artists, writers and other professionals whose work contributes to the quality of their games.

I would prefer to see AI used to support creative professionals rather than simply replace them.

Most importantly, I believe developers should be transparent about how they use generative AI and respect the rights of other creators.

My Advice to Other Game Development Students

If I were advising another student about intellectual property, my first suggestion would be to learn the basics of copyright before publishing their work.

Understanding asset licences, ownership and attribution could help prevent problems later.

I would also recommend keeping records of any third-party materials used during development.

For collaborative projects, I think it is sensible to discuss ownership and publishing permissions early rather than waiting until the project is finished.

Regarding generative AI, I would encourage students to experiment carefully without becoming dependent on automated tools.

AI can provide useful assistance, but I believe understanding the underlying programming, design and creative decisions is far more valuable for long-term development.

Finally, I would recommend following developments in copyright and AI regulation because the legal and ethical discussions are still evolving.

Conclusion

Overall, I believe intellectual property rights are essential for protecting creative professionals and encouraging original work within the games industry.

Copyright, trademarks, patents and design rights all serve different purposes, but together they can help developers protect the things they create.

However, I also believe these protections need to be balanced against the importance of creativity, competition and innovation.

Generative AI makes this balance even more complicated.

Although I can see genuine benefits in using AI to assist with programming, brainstorming and development, I remain concerned about ownership, consent and the potential consequences for artists.

As I continue developing my own games, I want to make sure I understand the rights associated with my work and respect the contributions of other creators.

Personally, I don't believe the future of game development should involve choosing between human creativity and artificial intelligence.

Instead, I would prefer an industry where new technologies help developers achieve more while ensuring that the people responsible for original creative work continue to receive recognition and fair treatment.

After all, what is the point of encouraging creativity if we fail to protect the people who make it possible?
